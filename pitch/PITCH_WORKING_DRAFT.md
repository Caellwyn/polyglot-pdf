## 1. The hook: put the audience inside the language barrier

Open by making the room *feel* the experience our users have every day, before we say a
word about the product. Most investors have never had to fill out a high-stakes legal or
medical form in a language they don't read.

Ideas for the opening 60 seconds:

- Put a real form on screen — an actual USCIS I-90 or I-589 page, or a hospital intake
  sheet — **in a language the room doesn't read** (e.g., the form rendered in Vietnamese
  or Arabic). Ask them to "fill in question 7." Let the discomfort sit for a beat.
- Frame the stakes: for our users this isn't a quiz, it's their green card, their
  asylum claim, their child's medical history. A wrong box can mean denial or deportation.
- Then the turn: "Now imagine you could just *talk* — in your own language — and the form
  fills itself out correctly. That's Polyglot."

The point of the cold-open is empathy, not information. The numbers come *after* they
feel the problem.

## 2. The problem, in numbers

> These are the figures to build the problem slide around. Sources are listed at the
> bottom. Use the conservative, well-sourced versions; round in speech, keep exact in
> the appendix.

**A large, growing population is locked out by language.**

- **~25.7 million people** in the U.S. — about **8%** of everyone age 5+, or roughly
  **1 in 12 residents** — have *Limited English Proficiency* (they speak English less
  than "very well"). (MPI / KFF, 2021 ACS; almost certainly higher today.)
- **More than 1 in 5 U.S. residents (~22%)** speak a language other than English at home.
- LEP is concentrated and growing — nearly **60%** of LEP residents live in just four
  states (CA, TX, FL, NY), and the LEP population grew ~80% from 1990 to the 2010s.

**The humans who bridge that gap are scarce and expensive.**

- Only about **75,300 interpreters and translators** are employed in the entire U.S.
  (BLS, 2024). That's roughly **one interpreter/translator for every ~340 people with
  limited English** — and most of that workforce isn't even doing community/medical/legal
  interpreting. *(Use as an illustration of structural scarcity, with that caveat.)*
- The field is growing only **~2% per decade** (slower than average), with **~6,900
  openings/year** — nowhere near enough to close the gap. There is a documented,
  worsening **shortage of certified medical interpreters**.
- **Cost to procure interpreting** (what an organization actually pays, not what the
  interpreter earns):
  - In-person: **$45–$150/hour**, typically with a **1–2 hour minimum**.
  - Certified medical interpreters: **$100–$140/hour**, often a **3-hour minimum**.
  - Phone interpreting: **$1.50–$4.00/minute**; healthcare video (VRI): **$1.95–$3.49/minute**.
- Interpreters themselves earn a median of **$59,440/year** (~$28.58/hr) — so the gap
  between what they cost to book and what they take home is largely scheduling friction
  and minimums.

**The consequence:** clients wait, appointments get rescheduled, and care/legal work is
delayed. Without proper interpretation, people face misdiagnoses, medication errors, and
denied or delayed services.

**Market context (for the "this is big" slide):** the global language-services market was
roughly **$75–78 billion in 2024**, projected to **~$111–132 billion by ~2033**.

## 3. The product, in one breath

Polyglot turns form-filling into a conversation. A client speaks naturally in their own
language (18+ supported), and Polyglot fills out a complex, real form — USCIS immigration
forms, medical intake, government benefits — accurately, with spoken confirmation of every
field, and generates a finished PDF. It's HIPAA-compliant by design (stateless, zero
storage of personal data) and runs on hardware as simple as an 8-inch tablet.

The key reframe for investors: **Polyglot is not a replacement for human interpreters —
it's the *first line of service* that lets each scarce, expensive interpreter help far
more people.**

## 4. The core argument: time saved → async service of many clients

This is the heart of the pitch. Today, one interpreter serves one client at a time,
synchronously, in real time. Polyglot breaks that 1:1 constraint.

**Today (synchronous, 1:1):** interpreter + client sit together for the whole form. The
interpreter is fully consumed for the entire session, and the client can't even start
until an interpreter is free — which can mean days or weeks of waiting.

**With Polyglot (async, 1:many):** the client completes the bulk of the form by voice, in
their own language, *whenever they want* — no interpreter needed for that step. The
interpreter only steps in at the end for a short review/QA pass. One interpreter can review
many clients' forms in the time a single sit-down used to take, and several clients can be
working in parallel.

### Illustrative time-and-cost model

> ⚠️ *Illustrative — replace every number with real pilot data before showing investors.*
> Inputs are deliberately adjustable so we can plug in observed values.

Assumptions:

- Synchronous interpreter-assisted form session today: **~90 min/client**.
- With Polyglot: client self-completes async (0 interpreter minutes) + interpreter does a
  **~20 min** review/QA pass per client.
- Blended procurement cost of interpreting: **~$100/hour**.
- A working interpreter has **~6 hours/day** of client-facing capacity.

Results:

| Metric | Today (1:1) | With Polyglot (first line of service) |
|---|---|---|
| Human interpreter time per client | 90 min | ~20 min (review only) |
| Interpreter cost per client | ~$150 | ~$33 |
| Clients served per interpreter-day | ~4 | ~18 (review-limited) |
| Client wait to *start* | days–weeks | immediate |

Takeaways to say out loud:

- **~78% less human interpreter time per client.**
- **~$117 saved per client** in interpreter cost.
- **~4–5x more clients served** by the same scarce interpreter — without burning them out.
- **Clients start immediately, in their own language,** instead of waiting for a slot.

The impact-investor version of this: *we don't have to magically produce 250,000 new
interpreters. We make the 75,000 we have go 4–5x further, and we give 26 million people a
way to start right now.*

## 5. Live demonstration plan

A live demo is the single most persuasive thing we can do — and the single most dangerous
if it breaks. **Rule #1: rehearse it cold, end to end, many times, on the actual room's
equipment.** Have a recorded backup video that we can cut to instantly if anything fails.

**Setup / signal chain:**

- Presenter wears a **lapel mic** → connects to the **laptop**.
- Laptop runs Polyglot and **broadcasts audio to the room speakers** so everyone hears
  both the participant and Polyglot's spoken responses.
- An **audience member interacts with the product** live — ideally someone who speaks a
  language other than English, picked/briefed in advance so it's authentic but reliable.
- Screen mirrors the form filling in real time so the room watches fields populate as the
  person speaks.

**The "wow" beat:** the volunteer speaks a sentence or two in their native language, and
the room watches a real government/medical form fill itself out correctly, with Polyglot
confirming each field back. Tie it directly to the cold-open: *this is the form you
couldn't fill out 5 minutes ago.*

**Rehearsal / risk checklist:**

- [ ] Test on the **venue's actual audio system and network** (or bring our own hotspot —
      never trust conference WiFi).
- [ ] Pre-select and brief the volunteer; have a fallback volunteer.
- [ ] Mic levels + speaker output dialed in; watch for feedback with the lapel mic.
- [ ] Pre-loaded form/schema; know exactly which fields we'll demo.
- [ ] **Recorded backup demo video** ready to play if anything fails.
- [ ] Offline/degraded-mode plan if the API or network drops.
- [ ] Time the whole thing — keep it tight (target ~2–3 minutes).
